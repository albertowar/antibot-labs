# Round 1 Answer Key (instructor)

Current to October 2026. These are the positions a careful practitioner would hold on the facts as written; a real case turns on the actual terms, the actual logs and the forum. Where a ruling is from one district court, or is on appeal, say so out loud: students should leave knowing which "rules" are settled and which are one judge's opinion.

| Case | Claim | Survives? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | CFAA | **No (weak)** | Public pages, no login: no gate was ever down. *Van Buren v. United States* (2021) reads "exceeds authorized access" as a gates-up-or-down question, and *hiQ v. LinkedIn* (9th Cir. 2022) applied that to public pages. A cease-and-desist letter does not by itself turn a public page into a gate. *Ryanair v. Booking.com* (D. Del. 2025) is the plaintiff's best hope, and it concerned account-gated content, under appeal, and still failed on the $5,000 "loss" element: engineering hours building defenses are not the kind of loss the statute counts unless tied to responding to the intrusion. |
| A | Contract | **Weak** | A footer "browsewrap" link that a logged-out visitor never clicked is hard to enforce. *Meta v. Bright Data* (N.D. Cal. 2024) held that Meta's terms bound users of the service, not logged-out visitors scraping public pages. A clear notice plus continued access after the letter helps, but it is a fight. |
| A | DMCA §1201 | **Depends** | *Reddit v. SerpApi et al.* (S.D.N.Y. July 2026) let a §1201(a) claim proceed on the theory that CAPTCHA and bot-detection systems are technological measures controlling access to copyrighted works. But the measure must protect a **copyrighted work the plaintiff owns or exclusively licenses**. Prices and stock facts are not copyrightable (*Feist*, 1991); product descriptions and photos may be. Google's parallel suit against SerpApi was dismissed, so the doctrine is unsettled and this is a district-court ruling, not appellate law. Surviving claim: narrow, limited to the copyrightable content behind the challenge. |
| A | Trespass to chattels | **Weak** | Requires actual impairment of the system (*Intel v. Hamidi*, Cal. 2003). 50k requests a day that the site served without degradation is not impairment. If the logs showed outages or measurable capacity costs caused by the scraper, this changes. |
| B | Contract | **Yes (strong)** | Clickwrap, accepted 400 times. *hiQ* itself ended with a consent judgment and permanent injunction on the **contract** claim after the CFAA claim failed. *Meta v. Bright Data* distinguishes logged-in use. Survival of terms after termination depends on the survival clause being clear (the Meta court found Meta's unclear), so read your own terms. |
| B | CFAA | **Plausible** | Accounts were terminated and access revoked: the gate is down and the scraper came back through it with new accounts. That is the *Van Buren* pattern the plaintiff wants. The $40,000 of documented incident-response cost is the kind of "loss" *Ryanair* found missing. Still a civil claim to prove, and circuits differ on how far revocation by letter reaches. |
| B | DMCA §1201 | **Depends** | Same analysis as Case A: the step-up challenge is plausibly a technological measure after *Reddit v. SerpApi*; the question is what copyrighted work it protected. Member pricing and inventory are facts. The recommendation feed may be a compilation with enough selection and arrangement to be protected. "Effectiveness" of the measure is not defeated by the existence of a cheap solver; courts have not required measures to be unbreakable. |
| B | Fraud | **Plausible** | Invented identities to obtain access the store would not have granted, plus a resale business built on it. Fact-heavy; the logs tying the 400 accounts to one operator are the evidence. |
| C | Contract | **Weak** | Same browsewrap problem as Case A, and Irish contract law adds its own questions. Not where this case is won. |
| C | CFAA-style | **No** | Public pages, no gate, and an EU defendant scraping an EU-facing site. US unauthorised-access claims are the wrong tool. |
| C | GDPR against Profilr | **Yes (strong)** | Profilr is a new controller processing personal data (names, photos, locations) with no lawful basis the reviewers would recognise, no Article 14 transparency, and a purpose (people search) the data subjects never contemplated. Clearview AI was fined in France, Italy, Greece, the Netherlands and the UK for exactly this pattern. The Global Privacy Assembly's joint statements on data scraping (2023, concluding statement October 2024) say in terms that **contractual permission to scrape cannot override privacy law**. Complaints from Irish and German customers give two supervisory authorities a reason to act. |
| C | "The store has no exposure" | **No** | This is the point of the case. The store is the original controller and left personal data on pages with no protection at all. The GPA statements tell platforms what regulators expect: rate limits, bot detection, blocking of known scrapers, monitoring for mass access, and telling affected users. "We did nothing" is not a defence; it is the finding. The store is also a candidate for a breach-notification question under Article 33 once the scraping is confirmed. |

## Scoring

12 claims. Strong or plausible survivals: B-contract, B-CFAA, B-fraud, C-GDPR (+1 each). "Depends": A-§1201, B-§1201 (+1 if the pair's reason mentions copyrightable content, 0 otherwise). Weak or no: A-CFAA, A-contract, A-trespass, C-contract, C-CFAA, C-"no exposure" (−1 if listed as surviving). Best possible: +6.

## What the detection logs would have needed to contain

Ask this after the reveal; it is the bridge to Round 2 and to the Capstone.

- **Case A:** a timestamped record of the challenge being served and solved programmatically (the §1201 "circumvention" fact), and the response cost to the business (capacity, engineering hours spent *responding*, not building). Most stores have neither.
- **Case B:** the link between the 400 accounts (shared fingerprints, shared infrastructure, the W2 Ch8 correlation signals), the exact moment of revocation, and every access after it. Plus the invoices.
- **Case C:** evidence that the store *had* protections proportionate to the data (a rate limit on review pages, bot detection, a scraping response plan). The logs here defend the store, not attack the scraper.

The uncomfortable conclusion: the same decision log that proves a scraper's wrongdoing is a store of personal data about every customer who was ever scored. Round 2 is about what that costs.
